# What made people fill out the intake form? — plain-language summary

**Date:** 2026-07-14
**For:** non-technical readers. The technical companion is
[`20260713_1030_f06_application_hazard_results.md`](20260713_1030_f06_application_hazard_results.md).

---

## What we did

We looked at roughly **39,000 text messages** sent to Ohio households between
January and March 2026 and asked: which texts were followed by someone clicking
the link and completing the energy-assistance intake form? **201 applications**
could be traced back to a specific text. We then compared the texts that led to
an application against the tens of thousands that didn't, while accounting for
where people live, when the text went out, and household differences.

The one-sentence version: **it's not what the message says or how many you
send — it's *when* in the campaign the message arrives.**

## What mattered (in order)

**1. The final week of a campaign is when people act — by far.**
Texts sent in the last week of a person's campaign produced about **150
applications per 10,000 texts**. Texts sent earlier produced about **4 per
10,000**. That's roughly a 40-fold difference, and it dwarfs everything else we
measured. Deadlines move people.

**2. The very first text also gets attention.**
A person's first-ever message (or the opening text of a new campaign) produced
about **80 applications per 10,000**, versus about 47 for messages in the
middle. First contact is novel; middle-of-campaign messages mostly get ignored.
Together, points 1 and 2 mean the *middle* of a campaign is the quiet zone —
the opening gets a bump, the closing week gets the flood.

**3. Cold weather probably helps — but we can't be sure yet.**
Applications were more common when texts landed during colder stretches (when
heating bills are biting). The pattern points the right way, but it comes from
only 12 campaigns in one winter, so we'd call it a promising hint rather than a
proven effect. Another coded winter of campaigns would settle it.

**4. Lower-income households applied more.**
A household earning around $25,000 was roughly a third more likely to apply
than one earning $60,000 — reassuring, since the program is meant for them.

**Things that made no detectable difference:** how many messages someone had
already received, how many days since the last one, and household size.

## What we could NOT answer (and why)

- **Email vs. text vs. mail.** Applications can only be traced back to a
  message when the link is tracked. Text links and mailed QR/web codes are
  tracked; email links are not. Email looks like it produced almost nothing,
  but that's because we're blind to it — not proof it doesn't work. (Fix:
  add tracking codes to email links.)
- **Message themes** (urgency, savings, reassurance…). The theme labels exist
  for older campaigns but were never applied to the 12 campaigns where we can
  trace applications. Labeling those 12 campaigns is a small task that would
  unlock this question.
- **News coverage of extreme weather.** We don't have that data; the
  temperature measurements stand in for it.

## One honest caution

We can only count applications that came from *clicking the tracked link in a
text*. Someone who got a text, then later phoned in or searched the website on
their own, isn't counted. So these results describe what triggers the
click-through path — the most common path we can see — not every possible route
to an application.

## What we'd do with this

1. **Front-load and back-load campaigns**: a strong opening message, a
   deliberately paced middle, and a clear final-week deadline push.
2. **Label the 12 traced campaigns with message themes** — cheap, and it
   answers the content question.
3. **Add tracking to email links** so the channel question becomes answerable.
4. **Repeat next winter** to confirm the cold-weather signal.
